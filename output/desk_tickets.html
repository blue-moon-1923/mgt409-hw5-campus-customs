Tauhid Zaman · customer_order · CC-TEE-WHITE, size S, qty 1 · carries invoice_id 501

Expected

Reflection

Written after the run of 14 October, with the ticket tabs and the cash tab open beside me.

How did the agents actually do?

Short version: one ticket was handled well, one was handled well and cheaply, and one was a mess that eventually got to the right answer. I'll take them in the order I'd rank them.

Ticket 102 was the best work on the board, and it's the one that looks least impressive. Four agent steps. Two passes. 37,507 tokens — a quarter of what ticket 101 burned. The boss called facilities and nobody else, and facilities did the one thing that actually mattered: it read the lease instead of believing the landlord. The ticket note from Elm City Properties says "shop rent due in 2 days", and on the closing pass the team's own reasoning reads "the lease record shows the next due date is 2026-10-02, contrary to the ticket's stated 2026-09-02". It went to the record. That is the difference between an assistant and a desk clerk who repeats whatever the email said.

It also caught itself on the second pass. The rent was already paid, and instead of asking me for $2,400 again it closed the ticket: "No further rent payment is needed… Ticket resolved without a duplicate payment request." Three of five agent plates stayed dark the whole time. Nothing was wasted.

Ticket 103 is the one I'd actually show someone. It resolved in a single pass, which none of the others managed, because declining is a complete answer — there was nothing to go back and check. It's the only ticket that used quote_bulk_price, which is the tool it exists for, and it found the money problem on its own: 8 hoodies on hand against 20 requested, $264 to reprint the missing 12, $152 in the account, $112 short. Nobody told it that. It also refused to promise twenty hoodies, which was the easy wrong thing to do.

One criticism, and it's real. The team quoted a specific discount — "up to 8 size-M hoodies at $52.20 each (10% below list)" — and nobody approved a 10% discount. It's a draft, so it hasn't gone anywhere, and the margin maths behind it is right ($52.20 against a $22 unit cost leaves $30.20 a hoodie, well clear of the floor). But a price is a commitment, and the shop has an approval gate for money going out and none for money coming in cheaper. That's a hole I built, not one the agents invented.

Ticket 101 is where it got ugly. Four passes. 474 seconds. 150,581 tokens — 60% of the entire run for a ticket about one $8 t-shirt. Look at the Actual section on that tab: nine hand-offs, thirteen agent steps, and the boss going back to inventory on three separate passes.

Worse, on pass 2 it asked me to pay for the reprint it had already placed. The duplicate-spend guard refused it and nothing was charged twice, but that guard only exists because an earlier run didn't have it and the shop paid $8 twice for the same shirt. And the drafts are bad. The first one says "Nothing has been ordered yet", the later one says "We're checking with our supplier to confirm whether your size S Bulldog tee order was placed." That is the shop telling a customer it doesn't know its own records — when the record plainly says payment 2, $8, Bulldog Print Co, 31 August. The team was more cautious than the data warranted, and excessive caution reads as incompetence to the person waiting for a t-shirt.

It got to the right place in the end. It just took four laps to do it.

Plan versus actual, ticket by ticket

I wrote the Expected sections before any of this ran, so this is a fair test. The honest scorecard: I got the routing right on all three and the process wrong on two.

101 — routing right, shape wrong. I predicted inventory first, then accounting, then customer service, with facilities never called. That is exactly what happened. What I completely missed is that it would take four passes. My plan assumed a ticket runs once, end to end — but a payment needs a human in the middle, so the real shape is: prepare, approve, come back. I also didn't foresee that coming back would make the team propose a spend it had already made. Nothing in my plan accounted for a second pass starting blind.

102 — the plan was right down to the optional bit. I wrote that accounting was "a judgement call, not a requirement" because facilities holds get_cash_position itself. The team judged it unnecessary and didn't call them. I also wrote that the landlord's "due in 2 days" was a claim rather than a fact and that the team should check the lease — it did, and said so in its own words. This is the one I'd point at if someone asked whether writing the plan first was worth it.

103 — right conclusion, and my arithmetic was out by $8. I predicted the escalation and predicted the gap. I said $104 short ($264 against $160). The team reported $112 ($264 against $152). We're both right about our own moment: I measured before any money moved, the team measured after the $8 tee had gone out on ticket 101. The shop was $8 poorer than my plan assumed, and the team's number is the live one. I'd rather be caught out by $8 than have fudged the Expected section after the fact.

I also predicted this one would need the most agents and it resolved fastest. Refusing is cheap.

What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

Two of the three tickets. Probably ticket 101 as well. I'm not going to pretend otherwise just because I built a team.

Ticket 102 should obviously have been one agent. The entire answer is: rent is $2,400, the lease says it's due 2 September, you have $3,400, do you want to pay it? One agent holding get_ticket_context and get_cash_position gets there in two tool calls and one reply. What we actually did was have a boss read the ticket, hand it to facilities, wait for facilities to read the ticket again and read the cash position, write a report, hand the report back, and then have the boss restate it. That cost 37,507 tokens and 244 seconds for an answer a single agent produces for a few thousand. The delegation bought nothing — look at the 102 tab: facilities used get_ticket_context and get_cash_position, which the boss could have called itself.

Ticket 101's second, third and fourth passes should have been one agent too. By then the question is "has the work been done, and can I close this?" — that is a lookup, not a conference. We spent three rounds of multi-agent delegation on it.

Ticket 103 is the one that earned the team. Here the separation did real work: inventory established the shortfall of 12, accounting established the $36 margin and the $152 balance, and the collision between those two facts is the $112 gap. Different skills, different tools, one finding neither would have reached alone. And it still only took one pass.

So where's the line? A team pays for itself when a ticket crosses domains and contains a conflict. Ticket 103 does: stock versus margin versus cash. Ticket 102 doesn't — it's one domain, one fact, one decision. Ticket 101 crosses domains on the first pass only; after that it's bookkeeping.

If I were doing this for a real shop I'd keep the specialists and make the boss much more willing to answer alone. Something like: read the ticket, and only delegate if it touches more than one specialist's area. That one rule would have taken 102 down to a few thousand tokens and saved most of 101's three follow-up passes — which together are most of the 249,481 tokens this whole run cost.

Three things this team could handle tomorrow, with the tools it already has

1. The Crest Mug is at zero and nobody has noticed. Check the stock table: CC-MUG-CREST, size OS, qty 0. Same situation as the tee on ticket 101, with one difference that makes it a better test — the vendor is Elm City Gifts, specialty "mugs and small goods", 3-day lead, and crucially not blocked, because the shop owes them nothing. The team has everything it needs: check_stock_and_restock_options would route past Bulldog Print Co on specialty and land on Elm City, get_cash_position would check the $152, and the order at $3.50 a mug is well within reach. The exact machinery that worked on 101 and 103, pointed at a different vendor.

2. A new supplier invoice lands while the shop is down to $152. This one is almost guaranteed — there are no open invoices left, so the next one is coming. Accounting already proved it can do this on ticket 103: read the balance, compare it to the claim, and refuse rather than overdraw. The cash floor would catch any payment over $152 the way it caught the $264 hoodie order, and the team would escalate instead of paying. And there's a second-order consequence it would already understand from ticket 101: an unpaid invoice blocks that vendor from shipping, so the escalation isn't "we owe someone money", it's "we can't restock from them until this clears".

3. Another student group asks for a bulk deal. Ticket 103's path, re-run. The Yale AI Club question needed quote_bulk_price to establish the $22 floor and the $58 list, then check_stock_and_restock_options to find out whether the quantity could be supplied, then the cash position to see whether a restock was fundable. All three tools exist, the margin logic is proven, and the team has already shown it will say "we can do eight, not twenty" rather than take the order and worry later.

Three things it would fall over on, and what it would need

1. "Did the tee actually arrive on 5 September?"

The team cannot answer this, and the reason is worse than it sounds. Look at the cash tab — payment 2 is stored as purchase_order, ref_id 1, $8.00. ref_id 1 is the vendor. The SKU, the size and the quantity are nowhere in that record. The shop knows it gave Bulldog Print Co $8 on 31 August and nothing else. That's also why ticket 101's drafts got vague — the team was right to hedge, because the record genuinely doesn't say what was ordered.

On top of that, place_restock_order deliberately does not touch inventory, because goods haven't arrived — but there is no tool that ever receives them, and desk.date_today never moves off 2026-08-31. The shop has no concept of time passing or goods landing.

Needs: a real purchase_orders table with sku, size, qty, vendor, order date, expected arrival and status; a receive_delivery(order_id, qty_received) tool that increments inventory and closes the order; and an advance_shop_date tool so tomorrow can exist. The inventory agent could own all of this — no new agent required, just tools and a table.

2. "Tauhid wants to return the tee."

Nothing in this system can take something back. There's no returns table, no way to put a unit back on the shelf, and cash in this shop is strictly one-directional — get_cash_position literally says "cash only goes out… no revenue is modelled". A refund is cash going out, so that part would work, but the stock would vanish into nothing and the customer's record wouldn't exist anywhere.

Needs: a returns table tied to a sale, a process_return tool that refunds and increments inventory in one transaction, and — this is the part people forget — a returns policy in the prompts so an agent knows what it may accept without asking. I'd give this to customer service with a hard approval gate: it can accept a return, but any refund over some threshold goes to a human, exactly like payments do now.

3. "We're down to $152. How do we make money?"

This is the big one, and it's a design hole rather than a missing tool. This shop has no revenue at all. Watch the cash tab: $3,400 goes to $152 and that's the whole story. Every ticket was about spending. Ticket 103 was literally a customer trying to give the shop money for twenty hoodies, and the only thing the team could do with that was work out what it couldn't afford to supply. A team that can only approve outgoings will always conclude "we can't afford it", because nothing ever arrives.

Needs: a sales table and a record_sale tool that decrements stock and increases cash; a sell-through rate so "will these twenty hoodies actually move?" is answerable; and then genuinely a sixth agent — call it Sales or Demand — that owns forecasting and pricing strategy rather than one-off quotes. Accounting can price a discount against cost, which is what it did on ticket 103. It cannot say whether giving the AI Club 10% wins repeat business worth more than the $47 of margin it gives up. That's a different job.

The thing I'd actually tell someone about this

The guardrails were worth more than the intelligence. Every genuinely good outcome on this board came from a rule that said no, not from an agent being clever: the cash floor refusing $264 against $152, the duplicate guard refusing the second $8, payments.approved_by being NOT NULL so nothing moves without a name on it. Three payments went out, all three say Christian Roberts, and the balance reconciles to $152.00 exactly.

And the failures were all process, not reasoning. The agents got the facts right nearly every time — 0 in size S, $840 overdue, $2,400 rent, $36 margin, $112 short. What went wrong was that a second pass couldn't see what the first had done, and that nobody had told the boss when a ticket is finished. Both of those were things I hadn't specified, and the team filled the gap with the most cautious guess available. Which, when I think about it, is exactly what a new hire does.
